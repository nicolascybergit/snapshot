The approach of the snapshot analysis…

Has this stock been here before?

By @nicolascybergit on 5 October 2026

Every trading day from the past leaves one specific picture behind for any stock or index that is worth looking at: For example, how far the price sat below its all-time high, whether it was moving sideways or breaking out into a certain direction, how high the trading volume was, and much more! The idea of the so-called ‘Snapshot Analysis’ works with exactly that picture and asks a plain question for every day in a stock's history. Did a day like the latest one already happen before, and if so, what came after it?

This is what the snapshot analysis is about. A day in the past still has something to tell us and provides a valuable outcome, because for that day we already know how its future went. We can simply look up how prices behaved in the days and weeks after it.

There is one rule that makes this analysis one of a kind: each snapshot day only uses information known from that very day or earlier. Only past data is allowed and nothing that happened later gets considered. It is very useful because consequently every day in the past is worth just as much as today. Thus, a snapshot from some time in 2017 knows just as much as you would have known on the evening of that very day, and no information beyond that.

From this foundation forward, every day in the past turns into a small example we can group and learn from.

The concept of a Block A and a Block B

Every snapshot consists of two halves. Block A describes the day itself, i.e., how far the price was below its all-time high, how many trading days had passed since that high, whether the stock was stuck in a plateau or in a breakout or had certain volume trades concluding in a set of many parameters that define the day. Block B then describes what happened in the future of that day, so how the price moved over the following trading days. This means how high or low it went and in what period of time, in order to calculate the velocity and the extent of the directional move. Block B, however, does consider data from that day’s future; that’s why it is important to distinguish between the two blocks.

With these two Blocks, A and B, which we calculate for every day from the past, we can start grouping the days only by their Block As. Block Bs stay out of this sorting completely, as what happened after a day can never decide which group a day lands in at first. We only open Block Bs afterwards, once groups are built, to see what followed their days.

This is the point where snapshot analysis operates very differently compared to conventional sequence or regression models such as Transformers, LSTMs/GRUs, Ridge regression, or ARIMA/GARCH. Snapshot doesn’t use a sequential forecasting architecture at all. A Transformer reads the data like one long story and predicts the future from that sequence. Since Snapshot operates in a very different way and restricts itself to past-only data, it could realistically challenge these conventional baseline models.

Back to our grouping mechanism: If the Block As of two days look alike, the two days end up in the same group and we could ask ourselves something along the lines of: ‘If these two days have almost the same conditions, then do their futures as well?’. That’s why the second step asks whether the Block Bs that are brought together because of the Block As agree within a group regarding a future outcome. If some days of that group were followed by rising prices and others by falling ones, that group tells us nothing interesting. That’s why we look only for groups whose futures match as well. In order to take it to the highest level possible, we could even implement a mechanism that observes the result of narrowing the Block Bs within a group and see if the Block As that didn’t match the Block Bs too well actually disappear or not. If so, we have more evidence about the fact that the futures of chosen Block As are even more promising.

Of course, a handful of groups for every stock and outcome is far too little to really predict a price securely, and this analysis isn’t supposed to work solely on its own. But it is an attempt to consider a new way of perceiving data and challenge traditional quant-trading methods. Combined with real-time market and news data, alongside macroeconomic data that can be built on top of the foundation later on, it could be worth far more than the foundation already is.
